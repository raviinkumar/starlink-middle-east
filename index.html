What to watch

  1. Saudi Arabia is the largest gap on the map. Aviation and maritime were approved in May 2025 and a local entity (Starlink KSA) exists, but there is still no residential licence from CST.
  2. Iraq's gateway dispute. The Communications Ministry argued in August 2026 that Iraqi traffic lands at a Qatar gateway; the regulator (CMC) denied it in late September and insists every terminal is registered and visible to security services. Expect pricing pressure too, at roughly $100/month entry.
  3. Iran is the only market where Starlink is both active (SpaceX enabled beams during the January 2026 blackout) and illegal, with terminals seized and the network declared a legitimate military target in April 2026.
  4. Lebanon holds a licence but has not activated service; the 2026 war makes a launch date unpredictable.
  5. UAE's second licence. Consumer service went live in March 2026 on the 2024 maritime licence; the 10-year General Space Services Licence issued 28 August 2026 puts it on firmer footing and opens government contracts.

From a rooftop dish to the open internet in under 40 milliseconds

Starlink is a mesh of thousands of small satellites 550 km up, talking to flat antennas on the ground and to each other by laser. Here's the path a packet takes, and where it is (and isn't) protected along the way.

How a packet gets there

Five hand-offs, four radio or optical hops, one place where the data is decrypted.

Dish finds a satellite

The flat antenna is a phased array: hundreds of tiny elements steer the beam electronically, no moving parts. It tracks a satellite crossing the sky in a few minutes, then hands off to the next.

Uplink 14.0–14.5 GHz, Ku band

Satellite receives and routes

Each satellite is a router in orbit. It decides whether to bounce the packet straight down to a nearby gateway or forward it through the mesh.

Downlink 10.7–12.7 GHz to users

Lasers cross the gaps

Where there's no gateway below (oceans, deserts, unlicensed countries), satellites pass traffic to each other over optical links until one is above a gateway.

100 Gbps+ per laser link

Gateway lands it

A fibre-connected earth station receives the Ka-band downlink, decrypts the Starlink link layer and hands the packet to a Point of Presence.

Ka band 17.8–19.3 GHz down

PoP joins the internet

The PoP applies carrier-grade NAT, assigns the public IP and peers with the wider internet. Return traffic follows the same path in reverse.

Public IPv4 via CGNAT, IPv6 prefix delegation

How it's secured, layer by layer

Starlink's model is "the operator encrypts and controls the link." That protects against eavesdropping on the airwaves; it does not remove SpaceX, or the gateway's host country, from the trust chain.

Link encryption, dish to gateway

Every radio hop and laser hop carries traffic encrypted with AES. Someone with a receiver pointed at the sky captures ciphertext, not your data.

Covers: dish → satellite → satellite → gateway

Terminal authentication

Each dish carries a unique device identity and certificate. The network only accepts sessions from registered hardware tied to an account, which is also how regulators (as in Iraq) can enumerate every active terminal.

Stops: cloned or unregistered dishes joining the network

Secure boot and signed firmware

The terminal will only run code signed by SpaceX, and firmware updates arrive over the encrypted link. Researchers who bypassed this in 2022 needed physical access and a voltage fault-injection board on the dish's circuit.

Stops: remote tampering with the dish's software

Carrier-grade NAT and no inbound by default

Your home network sits behind the PoP's NAT, so devices aren't directly reachable from the internet unless you pay for a public IP and open them up yourself.

Reduces: exposure of devices behind the router

Resilience through the mesh

Thousands of nodes and laser routing mean no single satellite, gateway or cable is a chokepoint, which is why the network kept working through wartime blackouts and jamming attempts.

Resists: single-point outages and localized interference

What the built-in encryption does not do

  • It ends at the gateway. Traffic is decrypted at the SpaceX earth station before it reaches the internet. Anything you want private end-to-end still needs HTTPS, a VPN or your own encryptor, exactly as on any ISP.
  • The keys belong to SpaceX. Governments and critical-infrastructure users treat this as operator-controlled encryption, and layer their own (increasingly quantum-resistant) encryption on top.
  • Jurisdiction follows the gateway. If the nearest gateway is in another country, your decrypted traffic first touches ground there. This is the substance of Iraq's 2026 dispute over a Qatar gateway.
  • Physical access is still game over. Like any device, a dish in an attacker's hands can be glitched into running custom code. SpaceX runs a bug bounty for exactly this research.
  • Availability can be switched off. SpaceX can geofence, throttle or disable terminals by policy or law, which cuts both ways for users in contested regions.
